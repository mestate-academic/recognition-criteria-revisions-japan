# Workers' Compensation for Overwork-Related Disease Following Revisions to Japan's Recognition Criteria, 2009–2025: An Interrupted Time Series Study

**Running title:** Recognition Criteria and Overwork-Related Disease

## ABSTRACT

**Objective:** To examine changes in the number and recorded overtime distribution of approved workers' compensation claims for cerebro-cardiovascular disease after Japan's 2021 revision of the recognition criteria.

**Methods:** Using national statistics for fiscal years 2009–2025, we fitted interrupted time series models to annual counts and compared counts of approved claims involving <80 versus ≥80 overtime hours per month within each year.

**Results:** The trend-adjusted level ratio for total approved claims was 1.11 (95% CI, 0.93–1.33). The ratio of approved claims involving <80 hours to those involving ≥80 hours increased 4.27-fold (95% CI, 3.28–5.57).

**Conclusions:** After the revision, the distribution of approved claims shifted toward lower recorded overtime. Evaluations of recognition criteria should monitor the categories that a revision addresses, and compensation statistics should report denied claims by the same categories.

**Keywords:** workers' compensation; occupational diseases; karoshi; overtime work; cardiovascular diseases; mental disorders; interrupted time series analysis

## LEARNING OUTCOMES

- Describe how Japan's 2021 revision clarified the assessment of overwork-related cerebro-cardiovascular disease when overtime falls below the "karoshi line" thresholds.
- Explain why the distribution of approved claims by recorded overtime can change even when no clear trend-adjusted level change is detected in their total number.
- Identify the category-specific indicators that a compensation system should monitor when its recognition criteria are revised.

## INTRODUCTION

Every workers' compensation system must decide which diseases count as work-related, and the rules it uses determine who is compensated. When these rules are restrictive, many workers with work-related illnesses receive no workers' compensation,¹ and cases are lost at successive stages between illness onset and claim approval.² The rules matter most for diseases with several causes, such as cerebro-cardiovascular disease (CCVD) and mental disorders. For these diseases, work-relatedness cannot be inferred from the diagnosis alone, so recognition criteria must specify which exposures and exposure levels are sufficient to establish work-relatedness. Long working hours are associated with a higher risk of coronary heart disease and stroke,³,⁴ and Japan, the Republic of Korea, and Taiwan have established explicit criteria for awarding compensation for CCVD attributed to overwork.⁵

Japan compensates overwork-related CCVD on the basis of overtime thresholds, and in 2021 it revised its criteria for the first time in 20 years. Since 2001, CCVD has been regarded as strongly work-related when overtime reached about 100 hours in the month before onset or averaged about 80 hours per month over the preceding 2 to 6 months. These thresholds are widely known as the "karoshi line"; *karoshi* means death from overwork.⁶,⁷ The revision, effective September 15, 2021, kept the thresholds but stated explicitly that overtime close to them, combined with other workload factors, could establish work-relatedness.⁸,⁹ For mental disorders, recognition criteria replaced earlier guidelines in 2011¹⁰ and were revised in 2020 to add workplace harassment involving a power imbalance ("power harassment")¹¹ and in 2023 to add harassment by customers and other events.¹² The revisions were intended to make adjudication clearer and faster.

Whether such revisions achieve their aims is rarely examined. Lin and colleagues reported that new CCVD criteria in Japan (2001) and Taiwan (2010) were followed by increases in the rate of compensated cases: 2.81-fold in Japan, 2.53-fold in Taiwan, and 2.58-fold in a pooled model.¹³ In Korea, approval rates for occupational CCVD fell and then rose again after successive changes to standards and procedures, but these changes were described rather than modeled.¹⁴ Outside East Asia, interrupted time series studies have evaluated compensation reforms in Australia,¹⁵,¹⁶ premium incentives in Finland,¹⁷ and presumptive coverage for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in Canada.¹⁸ Some of these studies examined subgroups, such as PTSD versus other mental health conditions,¹⁸ claim types,¹⁵ or claim severity.¹⁷ In Japan, administrative commentary has explained the 2021 revision,¹⁹ and studies have described approved cases;⁶,²⁰ however, to our knowledge, none of the revisions introduced in 2020, 2021, or 2023 has been evaluated quantitatively.

Totals can obscure a change that is confined to one category of cases. The 2021 revision addressed workers whose overtime fell below the thresholds. Over the same years, statutory caps on overtime, phased in from 2019,²¹ may have reduced the number of workers with very long hours. The distribution of approved claims by overtime could therefore change even if the total did not. This problem is not specific to Japan: compensation systems that use working-hour thresholds or presumptions of work-relatedness face it when those thresholds or presumptions are revised or reinterpreted. Using national statistics for fiscal years 2009 to 2025, we therefore examined whether the number of approved CCVD claims and their distribution by recorded overtime changed after the 2021 revision. As a secondary aim, we described compensation for mental disorders around the 2011, 2020, and 2023 revisions. Because the data are aggregate and no control population exists, we report associations with the timing of the revisions, not causal effects.

## METHODS

### Setting

Japan's Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance covers the employees of almost all private employers. Claims for overwork-related disease are filed with local Labour Standards Inspection Offices. Inspectors investigate working hours and workplace events and decide whether the disease is work-related by applying criteria issued by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW).

The 2021 revision of the CCVD criteria had four elements.⁹,¹⁹ First, overtime close to but below the thresholds could establish work-relatedness when other workload factors were present. Second, new workload factors were listed, such as rest intervals of less than about 11 hours between shifts and physically demanding work. Third, the criteria for short-term overload and extraordinary events were clarified. Fourth, severe heart failure was added to the list of compensable diseases.

The revisions of the mental disorder criteria are summarized in Supplemental Digital Content (SDC) 1, Table S1. Under these criteria, a single workplace event, such as severe harassment or violence, can independently warrant a rating of "strong" psychological stress. In that case, provided the other requirements of the criteria are met, the claim can be approved without an assessment of working hours. The criteria explicitly permit this approval pathway without requiring an investigation of working hours.¹⁰,¹²

### Data

We used the MHLW's reports on compensation for overwork-related disorders, which are published annually for the preceding fiscal year (FY; April to March).²² The reports provide national counts of claims, decisions (adjudicated claims, whether approved or denied), and approved claims for CCVD and mental disorders. Approved claims are also tabulated by the overtime hours established during the investigation. Overtime is not tabulated for denied claims.

*Approved CCVD claims by overtime.* Claims approved for long-term overload are reported in categories of average monthly overtime: <45, 45–<60, 60–<80, 80–<100, 100–<120, 120–<140, 140–<160, and ≥160 hours. Overtime is averaged over the applicable preonset assessment period: either 1 month (threshold, about 100 hours) or 2 to 6 months (threshold, about 80 hours per month). A separate category covers claims approved for short-term overload or extraordinary events. For FY2009–2019, we used tables that combine the two assessment periods, published by an expert committee.²³ Separate tables for each assessment period are available from FY2017; we summed the category counts across these tables for FY2020 onward. For FY2017–2019, these sums matched the combined tables.

*Approved mental disorder claims by overtime.* Approved claims are reported in categories of average monthly overtime during the psychological-stress assessment period (<20, 20–<40, 40–<60, 60–<80, 80–<100, 100–<120, 120–<140, 140–<160, and ≥160 hours), with an additional category for claims judged work-related "without the need to examine working hours" (FY2017–2025).

*Processing time for mental disorders.* We obtained mean times from claim filing to decision from expert committee materials for FY2009–2020²⁴ and MHLW policy evaluation documents for FY2021–2024.²⁵ The former reported time in months, which we converted to days by multiplying by 30; the latter reported time in days.

All values were transcribed by hand, with the source recorded for each value, and were checked against overlapping editions, annual totals, and the text of the reports. Values were available throughout the periods analyzed. Processing time for FY2025 had not been published and was therefore unavailable. Claims are counted by fiscal year of filing, and decisions and approved claims by fiscal year of decision. Since September 2020, the reports have tabulated a separate multiple-employment-factor category for workers whose combined workload across employers is assessed. Claim filings do not distinguish employment-injury claims from claims subsequently assessed under this category, whereas the decision and approval counts that we used exclude it (fewer than 10 approved CCVD claims in any year). The source of every series is listed in SDC 1, Table S2.

### Statistical Analysis

*Primary analysis: distribution of approved CCVD claims by overtime.* The primary estimand was the relative change in the ratio of approved claims involving <80 versus ≥80 overtime hours per month, comparing FY2022–2025 with FY2009–2020. Counts from the two assessment periods were combined. We selected 80 hours because it is the lower of the two overtime thresholds in the criteria and can be applied consistently throughout the study period; classification using the threshold specific to each assessment period was available only from FY2017. FY2021 was excluded as a transition year because the revision took effect in the middle of it. We fitted a quasi-Poisson model to the annual count in each category:

log E(count) = α(year) + β × Low + δ × (Low × Post),

where α(year) is a fixed effect for each fiscal year, Low indicates the <80-hour category, and Post indicates FY2022–2025; exp(δ) is the relative change in the ratio. The year fixed effects absorb factors that changed both categories by the same proportion in a given year, such as the overall volume of claims. They do not remove factors that affected the two categories differently. We assessed robustness to category-specific linear trends, alternative standard errors, changes in the years included, and placebo intervention dates. Alternative specifications used standard errors clustered by year or ordinary least squares models of the annual log ratio with a linear trend and Newey-West standard errors (lag 1).²⁶ Analyses using different years excluded each year in turn or shortened the prerevision period to FY2015–2020. The first set of placebo models used 4-year pseudo-postrevision periods beginning in each year from FY2012 through FY2017, compared with all preceding years from FY2009. The second set used pseudo-postrevision periods beginning in each year from FY2012 through FY2018 and ending in FY2020. Clustering by year allows for correlation between the two categories within a year but not for serial correlation across years.

*Totals.* We analyzed annual claims, decisions, and approved claims with quasi-Poisson segmented regression, and the approval proportion (approved claims divided by decisions) with quasi-binomial regression weighted by the number of decisions.²⁷⁻²⁹ Each model included a linear time trend and a postrevision indicator. The exponentiated indicator coefficient represents a level ratio for the count models and an odds ratio for the approval-proportion model. For CCVD, the models covered FY2009–2025, the postrevision period was FY2022–2025, and FY2021 was excluded. For mental disorders, the three revisions were modeled together over FY2009–2025, with indicators from FY2012, FY2020, and FY2024; FY2023 was excluded because the 2023 revision took effect in the middle of it, whereas the 2011 and 2020 revisions took effect near the end and the beginning of a fiscal year, respectively. The study period begins in FY2009 because decisions for mental disorders are available from that year. In sensitivity analyses, we changed the analytic window, removed the trend, coded the transition year differently, added a change in slope, and used ordinary least squares with Newey-West standard errors (SDC 1, Table S3).

*Assessment periods (exploratory).* Using the separate tables for FY2017–2025, we compared claims approved below the threshold for their own assessment period (<100 hours for 1 month; <80 hours for 2 to 6 months) with claims approved at or above it, and we examined each assessment period separately. These categories are defined operationally by the hour thresholds; the revision addressed overtime close to the thresholds in combination with other factors, not every case below a threshold. The separate tables cover only 4 years before and 4 years after the revision, and the contrasts use overlapping data, so they are not independent confirmation of the primary analysis. For the pooled below-versus-at-or-above-threshold contrast, we also fitted models with category-specific trends and calculated confidence intervals using year-clustered or Newey-West standard errors (SDC 1, Table S4). These analyses were added after critical review of an earlier draft and are post hoc.

*Other elements of the revision.* We compared claims approved for short-term overload or extraordinary events with those involving ≥80 hours. Because severe heart failure was added to the compensable diseases, we also compared approved claims for cardiac disease with those for cerebrovascular disease.

*Mental disorders (secondary).* For FY2017–2025, we used the same fixed-effects model with indicators beginning in FY2020 and FY2024. We estimated changes in two ratios: approved claims not requiring an assessment of working hours versus approved claims involving ≥80 hours, and approved claims involving <80 versus ≥80 hours. Processing time was described without modeling.

We report 95% confidence intervals without adjustment for multiple comparisons; all analyses other than the primary analysis should be interpreted as secondary or exploratory. Dispersion estimates are given in the table footnotes. Analyses were performed in Python 3 with the pandas and statsmodels packages. Reporting follows the STROBE and RECORD statements;³⁰,³¹ the completed checklist is provided as SDC 2.

## RESULTS

### CCVD: Totals

Comparing FY2009–2020 with FY2022–2025, mean annual counts were 826 versus 1028 for claims, 686 versus 693 for decisions, and 268 versus 217 for approved claims. The corresponding approval proportions were 39.1% and 31.3% (Table 1). In models with a linear time trend for each outcome, no clear change in level was detected for claims (rate ratio [RR], 1.13; 95% CI, 0.93–1.36), decisions (RR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.84–1.23), approved claims (RR, 1.11; 95% CI, 0.93–1.33), or the approval proportion (odds ratio [OR], 1.17; 95% CI, 0.90–1.51) (Table 2). Although the mean annual number of approved claims was lower after the revision, no clear level change was detected after adjustment for a common linear time trend. The confidence interval was compatible with changes ranging from a 7% decrease to a 33% increase. The estimate for the approval proportion depended on the analytic window (SDC 1, Table S3), and we regard it as uncertain.

### CCVD: Distribution by Overtime

The distribution of approved claims by overtime changed (Figure 1; Table 3). Mean annual approved claims involving <80 hours of overtime per month rose from 18.6 in FY2009–2020 to 48.5 in FY2022–2025, whereas those involving ≥80 hours fell from 230.9 to 141.0. Claims approved for short-term overload or extraordinary events rose from 18.9 to 27.5. The mean annual share of approved claims involving <80 hours rose from 7.0% to 22.5%.

The ratio of approved claims involving <80 hours to those involving ≥80 hours increased 4.27-fold (95% CI, 3.28–5.57). The estimate was 3.81 (95% CI, 2.22–6.53) with category-specific trends, 4.27 (95% CI, 2.95–6.20) with standard errors clustered by year, and 3.81 (95% CI, 1.73–8.37) when both were used. The ordinary least squares model of the annual log ratio, with a linear trend and Newey-West standard errors, yielded an estimate of 3.88 (95% CI, 2.33–6.48). When each year was excluded in turn, the estimate ranged from 4.01 to 4.60, and it was 4.54 (95% CI, 3.11–6.63) when the prerevision period was shortened to FY2015–2020 (Table 3; SDC 1, Table S5). In the placebo models with 4-year windows, estimates ranged from 0.71 to 1.12, and one of the six confidence intervals excluded 1 (FY2015–2018: 0.71; 95% CI, 0.53–0.94). In the second set of placebo models, estimates ranged from 0.90 to 1.33, and all confidence intervals included 1 (SDC 1, Table S6).

The ratio of claims approved for short-term overload or extraordinary events to those involving ≥80 hours also increased (relative change, 2.38; 95% CI, 1.46–3.89). No clear change was detected in the ratio of cardiac to cerebrovascular cases (0.98; 95% CI, 0.86–1.11).

### CCVD: Assessment Periods

Table 4 and Figure 2A show the two assessment periods separately for FY2017–2025. Among claims assessed over 1 month, those involving ≥100 hours fell from a mean of 75.8 per year in FY2017–2020 to 44.3 in FY2022–2025, and those involving <100 hours rose from 3.8 to 17.3. Among claims assessed over 2 to 6 months, those involving 80–<100 hours fell from 82.3 to 48.3 per year, and those involving <80 hours rose from 17.0 to 42.5. Claims involving ≥100 hours in this group changed little (33.8 and 37.3), as did the group total (133.0 and 128.0).

Claims approved below the threshold for their assessment period accounted for 7.1%–12.0% of all approved claims in FY2017–2020 and 22.7%–33.5% in FY2022–2025. Their ratio to claims approved at or above the thresholds increased 4.26-fold (95% CI, 2.84–6.37). The estimate was less precise when each category had its own trend (3.60; 95% CI, 1.25–10.35), and its confidence interval included 1 when standard errors were also clustered by year (95% CI, 0.50–25.87; SDC 1, Table S4). With only 4 years on each side of the revision, these analyses are descriptive; they are consistent with the primary analysis but do not confirm it independently.

In the combined tables, no clear change was detected in the ratio of approved claims involving 80–<100 hours to those involving ≥100 hours (0.96; 95% CI, 0.81–1.13; Figure 2B). Mean annual counts decreased by similar proportions in the two categories, from 99.8 to 59.5 and from 131.1 to 81.5, respectively. The estimate was similar when the prerevision period was limited to FY2017–2020 (0.93; 95% CI, 0.75–1.15). The separate tables show that this combined ratio conceals different patterns in the two assessment periods. Among claims assessed over 2 to 6 months, the ratio fell (0.53; 95% CI, 0.40–0.71), because claims involving 80–<100 hours decreased while those involving ≥100 hours did not. Among claims assessed over 1 month, the decrease was in claims involving ≥100 hours.

### Mental Disorders

Claims rose from 1136 in FY2009 to 4958 in FY2025, and approved claims from 234 to 1082 (Figure 3A; Table 1). After the 2011 criteria were introduced, the approval proportion increased (OR, 1.63; 95% CI, 1.34–1.99), and no clear change in claims was detected (Table 2). After the 2020 revision, approved claims increased (RR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.06–1.40). The estimates for the 2023 revision depended on how the trend was specified. In the main model, claims (RR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.26–1.63) and approved claims (RR, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.37–1.74) were higher after the revision. When the slope was allowed to change from FY2020, the estimate for claims was 1.00 (95% CI, 0.85–1.18) and that for approved claims was 1.32 (95% CI, 1.02–1.71) (SDC 1, Table S3). The estimated level change in claims was thus not robust to allowing a slope change from FY2020. The estimate for approved claims was attenuated but remained above 1; the short follow-up and the sensitivity to trend specification limit attribution to the 2023 revision.

Most of the growth was in claims approved without an assessment of working hours. Mean annual counts increased from 139 in FY2017–2019 to 315 in FY2020–2022 and 657 in FY2024–2025. These claims accounted for 24%–31% of all approved claims in FY2017–2019 and 58%–65% in FY2024–2025 (Figure 3C; Table 5). Approved claims involving ≥80 hours and those involving <80 hours each stayed between about 150 and 230 per year. Mean processing time was approximately 255 days in FY2011 and 216 days in FY2015–2017, compared with 277–278 days in FY2023–2024 (Figure 3D). The recent values exceed both the 8-month standard processing period (about 240 days) and the administrative target of 215 days.²⁵ Annual decisions increased from 1355 in FY2016 to 3496 in FY2024. Approved claims involving suicide or attempted suicide stayed between 63 and 99 per year.

## DISCUSSION

### Main Findings

After Japan's 2021 revision of the CCVD criteria, the distribution of approved claims by recorded overtime changed, but no clear trend-adjusted level change was detected in their total number. Approved claims involving <80 hours of overtime per month rose 2.6-fold, and their ratio to those involving ≥80 hours rose about 4-fold. This estimate was stable when single years were excluded, and placebo models within the prerevision period gave estimates between 0.71 and 1.33. In both assessment periods, approved claims involving overtime below the applicable threshold increased. Among claims assessed over 1 month, those involving overtime at or above the threshold decreased; among claims assessed over 2 to 6 months, those just above the threshold (80–<100 hours) decreased. For mental disorders, claims approved without requiring an assessment of working hours increased after the revisions. Processing times in FY2023–2024 were longer than in the mid-2010s, but only 1 complete year of processing-time data was available after the 2023 revision.

### Possible Explanations for the Change in CCVD Claims

At least three explanations are possible, and they are not mutually exclusive.

The first is wider eligibility. The revised criteria may have enabled approval of claims from workers with overtime below the thresholds and other workload factors whose claims might previously have been denied. This would be the intended effect of the revision.

The second is a reduction in working hours. Overtime caps introduced in April 2019 (April 2020 for small and medium-sized employers) limit overtime, including work on statutory holidays, to less than 100 hours in any single month and to an average of no more than 80 hours over 2 to 6 months.²¹ These limits are close to the thresholds of the criteria. From April 2019, employers were also required to record working hours objectively.³² If overtime fell to just below the thresholds among workers who subsequently developed CCVD, the revision may have maintained their access to compensation rather than extended eligibility to new groups. Because decisions are made some time after onset, an effect of the caps could overlap in time with the revision. There is, however, a reason to doubt that the caps explain the whole change. The caps took effect in the construction sector and for professional drivers only in April 2024. Professional drivers are subject to an annual overtime limit of 960 hours rather than the monthly and multimonth limits,³³ yet road freight transport accounts for the largest share of approved CCVD claims.²²

The third is a change in how hours were recorded. Changes in the documentation or assessment of overtime may have shifted the distribution of approved claims across the recorded overtime thresholds without corresponding changes in actual hours or eligibility.

The number of approved claims involving <80 hours more than doubled, so the change in the ratio was not produced solely by a decline in the reference category. The prerevision share of such claims also showed no sustained upward trend (range, 4.8%–10.6% in FY2009–2020). These observations do not, however, distinguish among the three explanations. Among claims assessed over 2 to 6 months, the decrease was concentrated just above the threshold, whereas counts involving ≥100 hours and the total count changed little. This pattern is compatible with a shift in recorded overtime from just above to just below the threshold. It is also compatible with wider eligibility combined with fewer workers recording 80–<100 hours. Other events during this period, including the COVID-19 pandemic, may also have affected the overtime categories differently. Distinguishing among these explanations would require counts of denied claims stratified by recorded overtime or individual claim records.

### Mental Disorders

The concentration of growth in claims approved without an assessment of working hours is consistent with the focus of the revisions on harassment, because a severe harassment event can satisfy the occupational psychological-stress requirement without consideration of working hours, provided the other recognition requirements are met. The same pattern could, however, arise in other ways: from greater awareness after legislation required large employers to implement measures to prevent power harassment from June 2020 and small and medium-sized employers from April 2022,³⁴ from wider use of this pathway by investigators facing rising caseloads, or from recordings and electronic messages that make harassment easier to substantiate. Our data cannot separate these mechanisms. Processing times were longer in recent years, during the expansion of this approval category. However, data were available for only 1 full year after the 2023 revision, and the contributions of investigative complexity and rising caseloads could not be determined.

### Comparison With Previous Studies

Lin and colleagues found that new CCVD criteria in Japan in 2001 and in Taiwan in 2010 were each followed by a more than 2.5-fold increase in the rate of compensated cases.¹³ Japan's 2021 revision was followed by a change in the distribution of approved claims without a clear trend-adjusted level change in their total number. Durand-Moreau and colleagues found that presumptive coverage restricted to PTSD in Canada was associated with a higher proportion of PTSD claims among all lost-time claims, but not with a change in the corresponding proportion for other mental health conditions.¹⁸ In both settings, the change was concentrated in the category that the rule addressed. Our analysis by assessment period adds a caution: a change in one category can be accompanied by an opposite change in the adjacent category, so adjacent categories should be examined together.

### Implications

The 2021 revision kept the existing hour thresholds and clarified that overtime close to them, together with other workload factors, could support recognition. After this clarification, the distribution of approved claims across the overtime categories changed substantially, although no clear trend-adjusted level change was detected in their total number. Totals alone would not have shown this change.

Many jurisdictions use working-hour thresholds, as in Korea and Taiwan, or occupation-specific presumptions, as in North America and Australia, to assess compensation claims for multifactorial diseases. For such systems, our findings suggest three practical steps. First, a revision should be evaluated by the categories it addresses and by the adjacent categories, not only by total approved claims or approval proportions. Second, systems should routinely monitor the distribution of approved claims by overtime, the share approved without an assessment of hours, and processing time. Third, systems should publish the number of denied claims by recorded overtime, so that approval proportions can be calculated for each category.

Further research should assess whether investigative capacity has kept pace with the increasing number of mental disorder claims. For occupational physicians, the revised criteria mean that a worker who develops CCVD after substantial overtime below the thresholds may still be compensated when other workload factors are present. Documenting these factors, such as short rest intervals between shifts, in workplace health records may therefore help workers and adjudicators.

### Strengths and Limitations

The study used complete national statistics spanning 17 years, distinguished claim filings from decisions and approved claims, and analyzed the two assessment periods separately. The data and code are publicly available.

The study has several limitations. First, the data are aggregate. We could not follow individual claims, and hours are tabulated only for approved claims, so approval proportions by overtime category could not be calculated. Second, the overtime categories reflect the hours established in the investigation, which may be underestimated and may themselves change after a revision. Third, there was no control series, because all claims in Japan are decided under the same criteria, and several events coincided with the revisions: the overtime caps, the requirement to record working hours, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the harassment-prevention legislation. We therefore report associations, not causal effects. Fourth, the series are short and may be serially correlated. With 16 annual observations, the alternative standard errors are themselves imprecise. The assessment-period tables and the mental disorder categories begin in FY2017, and follow-up after the 2021 and 2023 revisions was 4 and 2 years, respectively. Fifth, we made many comparisons without adjustment for multiplicity, and the assessment-period analyses were post hoc. Sixth, the combined CCVD tables came from different sources before and after FY2020, although they matched in the overlapping years. Finally, the findings come from one country, and the size of any change after a revision will depend on how each system defines its thresholds.

## CONCLUSIONS

After Japan's 2021 revision of the recognition criteria for overwork-related CCVD, the distribution of approved claims shifted toward lower recorded overtime. The ratio of approved claims involving <80 hours per month to those involving ≥80 hours increased about 4-fold, whereas no clear trend-adjusted level change was detected in the total number of approved claims. Aggregate statistics cannot show whether the revision extended compensation to newly eligible workers or maintained it for workers whose recorded hours fell. Evaluations of recognition criteria should examine the categories that the criteria address together with the adjacent categories, and compensation statistics should report denied claims by the same categories.
